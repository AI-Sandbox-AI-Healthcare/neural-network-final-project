Benchmarking Machine Learning Models on Synthetic Clinical Records

This project benchmarks seven machine learning models on a binary patient classification task using 12,352 synthetic patients generated by Synthea. We compare models ranging from logistic regression to a fine-tuned ClinicalBERT transformer. Each model uses patient information, including demographics, vitals, medications, and encounter notes. We evaluate performance and computational cost across 30 iterations with different random seeds.

🏥
Clinical Context
Chronic pain affects roughly 20% of US adults and is a leading cause of disability and healthcare spending. Electronic health records hold the diagnoses, medications, and vitals needed to study it at scale, but privacy rules limit access to real patient data. Synthetic records offer a safe way to build and test methods.
🎯
Research Question
Healthcare teams must choose between simple, interpretable models and large deep learning models that need GPUs and specialized infrastructure. Which machine learning architecture offers the best trade-off between classification performance and computational cost, and when is the added complexity actually worth it?
🔬
Scientific Rigor
Many machine learning results in healthcare come from a single train/test split and are hard to reproduce. A model can look better because of a lucky split, a leaky feature, or noisy labels. Repeating every experiment across 30 random seeds on shared splits shows which differences are real and which are chance.

🖥️ CPU Baseline
Tabular & Text
Random Forest and XGBoost use 13 structured features (demographics, costs, vitals). TF-IDF Logistic Regression reads each patient’s raw medication text. All three train in a few seconds per iteration on CPU.
F1 range: 0.649 – 0.699
⚡ GPU Sequence
Sequence Models
LSTM, GRU, and a custom Transformer take structured and medication-text features as 10-step sequences. The feature table has one row per patient, so each sequence is left-padded and holds a single real time step. 14–22 s per iteration on an A100.
F1 range: 0.679 – 0.692
🧠 Pretrained BERT
Clinical Language Model
Bio_ClinicalBERT, pre-trained on real clinical notes (MIMIC-III), turns each patient’s medication text into an embedding once. A small Transformer head learns from that embedding plus structured features. Scored on the 1,445 test patients who have medication records. ~36 s per iteration.
F1: 0.707 (highest mean F1)
The Problem We Are Solving
Every model in this project answers the same yes-or-no question: does this synthetic patient have a pain-related diagnosis? Supervised learning needs that answer, called the label, for every patient. Health records do not include a ready-made “has chronic pain” field, so we build the label ourselves from the diagnoses in conditions.csv. How we build it shapes what every model learns and what its scores really mean.
How We Create Labels
We create patient-level labels by searching diagnosis descriptions for 31 pain-related keywords, such as “pain,” “arthritis,” “migraine,” “fracture,” and “injury.”
Positive (1) At least one diagnosis contains a matching keyword.
Negative (0) No diagnosis contains a matching keyword.
This produces 6,235 positive patients (50.5%) and 6,117 negative patients (49.5%).
The labels are simple and reproducible but imperfect. They include chronic conditions, temporary injuries, and some unrelated diagnoses. Because the model features come from the same patient history used to create the labels, they may contain information that directly reveals the label.

More Insights on the Classification Label
Keyword labels are quick and transparent, which is why they are common in health-records research. A closer look at ours shows a few things to keep in mind before you trust any model score.
🫁 Some matches are not about pain
Keyword matching is noisy. Some diagnoses contain a keyword but have nothing to do with pain, so those patients get the wrong label. For example, “chronic” also matches chronic sinusitis, chronic kidney disease, and chronic heart failure.
🩹 Temporary problems count as much as chronic pain
A sprained ankle that has healed counts the same as fibromyalgia. “Joint pain” and “muscle pain” each appear in 1,352 patients, all of whom had COVID-19, and 93% of those records are marked resolved. 1,225 positive patients (19.6%) have only short-term problems.
📈 The label grows with age
Every diagnosis in a patient’s lifetime counts, so older patients have more chances to match a keyword. A model can score well partly by learning age.

19 Features from Medication Names
Join each patient’s medication names into one “medication note.” From it, compute TF-IDF weights for 12 pain-related drug names, 5 LDA topics (themes in medication use), a sentiment score, and a K-means cluster number. The 2,786 patients with no medications get 0 for all of these.

Which Baseline Features Matter?
How much the Random Forest’s test AUC drops when one feature’s values are shuffled, averaged over the 30 seeded splits. A bigger drop means the model relies on that feature more.
Age and insurance coverage carry most of the signal. Age matters partly because older patients have more diagnoses and are more often labeled positive, as the Dataset tab shows.
Room to Improve
Because the baseline is deliberately simple, there are clear places to start.
🧩 Add more features
The baseline is deliberately simple, so there is plenty of room to improve it. Explore the data, follow your own ideas, and build features you can explain. Every feature you add is a chance to beat the benchmark.
⚠️ Watch for leakage
Avoid features built from conditions.csv or from REASONDESCRIPTION columns, which repeat the diagnoses behind the label. If a new feature makes the score jump, check whether it is quietly encoding the label.

Core ML Practice
Reproducibility

ClinicalBERT reached F1 0.730 in its luckiest run, but across all 30 runs it averages 0.707 ± 0.014. Reporting only the lucky run would overstate it by 0.023. The 30-run average is the number you can trust. Reproducibility is the difference between a result and a finding.

🎲
Random Seed
Controls how data is split and how models initialize. Different seeds = different results.
📊
Standard Deviation
How much results vary across runs. Low std = stable model. High std = sensitive to split luck.

The core lesson
1
Never report a single run. Here, each model's best run beats its 30-run average by 0.02 to 0.035.
2
Share your random seed. Anyone re-running your code should get the same number.
3
Report mean ± std. The spread tells readers how confident they should be in your result.
4
Use shared splits. All 7 models here use the same 30 train/validation/test splits — that's why their comparisons are fair.

Tasks — experiments to reflect on in your report
📌
These tasks change your model on purpose: less training data, removed features, missing values. They are for reflection in your report only. Do not upload their results; my_model.csv is for your best model.
01
How Much Training Data Does Your Model Need?

Design your model, then train it three times: on 1,000, 4,000, and all 8,646 training patients. Keep the same validation and test patients for every run, so the only thing that changes is the amount of training data.

  1. Before each run, write down what you expect to happen and why.
  2. Run your model.
  3. Compare the result with your expectation and explain any gap.
It is okay to be wrong. If your expectation missed, explain why you were wrong and what you learned.
02
Which Features Drive Your Model?

Find out which features your best-performing model relies on most, then test what happens without them.

  1. Identify the top features contributing to your best-performing model, and explain why you think they matter most.
  2. Remove the top two features and retrain your model.
  3. Report the new results.
Explain your updated results and the reasoning behind them. Did performance change the way you expected, and what does that tell you about the remaining features?
03
How Robust Is Your Model to Missing Data?

Real health records are often incomplete. Simulate this by hiding values in your most important features.

  1. Randomly set 50% of the values in each of your top 3 features to missing. Use a fixed random seed so you can reproduce it.
  2. Before running, predict how your model’s performance will change and why.
  3. Fill the missing values using a statistic, such as the mean or median, computed on the training set.
  4. Run your code and report the results.
Compare the results with your expectation and explain any gap. It is okay to be wrong; reason about why and what you learned.
